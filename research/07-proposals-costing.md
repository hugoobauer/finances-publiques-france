# 07: Costing major party proposals against France's deficit and debt

*Research file for the public-finance education platform. Status as of 2026-10-25. All amounts are in billions of euros (bn EUR) at current prices unless stated otherwise.*

---

## 0. Scope and definitions

**What this file covers.** It sets out the budgetary yield or cost of the main fiscal proposals in French political debate. That means wealth taxes, the minimum tax on high incomes, the return of the ISF, price freezes and VAT cuts, pension age, the minimum wage (SMIC), civil-service pay and anti-fraud measures. It also covers the aggregate costings of party programs: the 2024 legislative programs, and the 2027 presidential packages announced as of 2026-10-25. Each figure is compared with three things:

- the **2025 public deficit**: **151.6 bn EUR, 5.1% of GDP** (October 2026 EDP notification, Eurostat/Insee, and PLF 2027; Insee's May 2026 vintage gave 152.5 bn). Shares of the deficit below were computed on 152.5 bn; the difference is under 0.6% and does not change any rounded share;
- the **2026 deficit forecast**: **164.9 bn EUR, 5.4% of GDP** (PLF 2027 article liminaire). The "~160-170 bn" in the brief matches the 2026 forecast and the 2024 outturn (169.1 bn), not the 2025 outturn;
- **total public expenditure 2025**: **1,714.2 bn EUR, 57.3% of GDP** (Insee, including tax credits; Eurostat's October 2026 release gives 1,714.1 bn, the canonical value in file 00 row 1; the 0.1 bn difference changes no ratio below). Excluding tax credits (*hors crédits d'impôt*, the convention used by the government and HCFP) it is 1,694 bn EUR, 56.6% of GDP.

**Key definitions**

| Term | Meaning used here |
|---|---|
| *Déficit public* (public deficit) | Net borrowing (B.9) of general government (S13), ESA 2010 national accounts ("Maastricht" deficit). Not the State budget deficit (*déficit budgétaire de l'État*), which is a cash-basis concept covering only the central State. |
| % of GDP vs % of total spending | Every ratio below says which one it is. "% of deficit" means the share of the 2025 Maastricht deficit, computed on the May 2026 vintage (152.5 bn); the canonical value is 151.6 bn (file 00 row 31), and no rounded share changes. "% of total spending" means the share of 2025 total public expenditure incl. tax credits (1,714.2 bn). |
| Static / mechanical costing | Yield = rate × current base, with no behavioral response (the method used by Institut Montaigne, by party costings, and by Zucman's headline figure). |
| Behavioral / dynamic costing | Allows for base erosion (avoidance, timing, emigration, income shifting) and, where noted, macro feedback. |
| *Prélèvements obligatoires* (PO, compulsory levies) | Taxes plus actual compulsory social contributions, net of tax credits (Insee definition). |
| *Effort structurel* vs. "quantum of measures" | The HCFP measures fiscal effort against the previous year (structural effort). The government reports measures against a "no-policy-change" trend (*tendanciel*), which gives larger numbers. They are not interchangeable (see §2). |

**Reading guide.** Read "confidence" in the summary table like this. **High** means an institutional estimate (HCFP, Cour des comptes, Insee) or an observed outturn. **Medium** means an independent costing (IPP, CAE, Institut Montaigne) with a stated method. **Low** means a party's own claim, a contested estimate, or one found only in secondary press.

## 3. Proposal-by-proposal analysis

### 3.1 The "taxe Zucman" (2% minimum tax on wealth above 100 M EUR)

**Description.** This is an annual *impôt plancher* (floor tax): households with net wealth above 100 M EUR, **including business assets**, would pay at least 2% of their wealth, minus taxes they already pay (income tax, social levies, IFI). It was introduced as bill n° 768 by Eva Sas (Ecologists) and Clémentine Autain on 2025-01-07, with an exit-tax clause keeping taxpayers liable for 5 years after they leave France [S4].

**Supporters.** All four left-wing groups (LFI, PS, Ecologists, GDR). LFI's 2027 package counts it at 15 bn [S18]. Economists who backed the principle include Olivier Blanchard and Jean Pisani-Ferry (*Le Monde* op-ed with Zucman, June 2025) and seven Nobel laureates in a separate op-ed [S26]. Blanchard explained his support on the grounds that "the very rich use tax optimization much more than others" [S42].

**Opponents.** The government, Renaissance, MoDem, Horizons, LR and UDR. The RN opposes this design but favors a different wealth tax, an *impôt sur la fortune financière* (tax on financial wealth) [S19], [S23].

**Constitutional risk.** The Senate report notes that the Conseil constitutionnel has accepted a 0.5% uncapped wealth-tax rate (decision 2011-638 DC). It upheld a 1.8% top rate only with a ceiling mechanism linked to income (2012-662 DC). A 2% rate with no ceiling therefore risks being struck down as confiscatory [S4]. Some jurists dispute that the 2012 case law would be applied mechanically ([S26], citing a *Le Monde* op-ed). This is a **genuine legal uncertainty, not a settled point**.

**Behavioral issues specific to this design.** (i) The base includes business assets. Earlier French and Swedish exile estimates come from wealth taxes that excluded them, so they may understate the response (A. Lévy, cited in [S4]). The CAE answers that Danish evidence, where business assets were included, gives similar elasticities [S5]. (ii) Liquidity: owners of low-dividend or loss-making firms would have to sell shares or pay in kind [S4]. (iii) Valuing unlisted firms every year is hard [S4].

**"Zucman light" and the 2026 holding tax.** The government's PLF 2026 proposed a 2% tax on non-operating assets held in personal holding companies (*holdings patrimoniales*). The Senate cut its base to sumptuary assets (yachts, collector cars, racehorses, jewelry) held by holdings of individuals with ≥ 5 M EUR of assets, at a 20% rate. It was upheld by the Conseil constitutionnel on 2026-02-19 and is first payable in spring 2027 ([S25], secondary legal press). Its expected yield is reported as **~0.1 bn EUR**, against "over 1 bn" (1-1.5 bn in one account) for the original 2% version ⚠ unverified (secondary press quoting a private-bank analyst; **no official annex or parliamentary report found** on 2026-10-25). The IPP's analysis of the holding tax declined to give a figure [S9]. Note: an AN amendment of 6 Jan 2026 (n° 48 on PLF 2026, text n° 2247) describes the Senate text as covering non-operating **real estate as well as** sumptuary assets, so "sumptuary assets only" may understate the base. **Recommendation: keep with caveat** ("yield expected to be small, around 0.1 bn according to press reports; no official estimate").

**Assessment for the platform.** Even the author's central 20 bn covers about **one-eighth of the 2025 deficit** and **~1.2% of public spending**. A post-behavior figure of 5 bn covers about **3%** of the deficit. The yield also has a fat tail on the downside, because a handful of taxpayers make up most of the base.

**Exit-tax and emigration debate.** The CAE (Focus 118, July 2025, authors Bach, Bozio, Grimprel, Guillouzouic, Landais, Malgouyres) finds that the 2013 capital-income tax increase raised net departures by at most 0.09 pt, i.e. **fewer than 350 additional net departures a year** out of the 385,000 households in the top 1% of capital income (press release) (corrected after verification: the 350 figure is the *extra* departures induced by the 2013 tax shock, upper bound, not the total number of departures). The 2013 tax increase raised the net departure rate of the most affected by 0.04-0.09 pt, and the 2017-2018 cuts lowered it by 0.01-0.07 pt. The long-run "stock elasticity" is 0.02-0.18%, i.e. 0.4-3.6× the Swedish estimate [S5]. When a large shareholder leaves, the affected firm's turnover falls by about 15% and its payroll by about 30%. Aggregated, though, a 4 bn reform would cost at most about 0.03% of national turnover [S5]. **Implication:** ISF-type taxes are not self-defeating through exile. They are simply small relative to the deficit: about 3% of it.

**Dividends.** The France Stratégie committee found that higher dividend payouts after the flat tax (24 bn more over 2018-2020) offset the static cost of the PFU (~1 bn) [S4]. This is evidence that capital-income tax bases are highly elastic, and the same elasticity cuts both ways for proposals to repeal the PFU.

### 3.3 Taxing high incomes: the CDHR as a real-world test

The **CDHR** (*contribution différentielle sur les hauts revenus*, a 20% minimum tax on reference income above 250,000 EUR for single filers and 500,000 EUR for couples) was created by the 2025 finance law and extended in LF 2026 until the deficit falls below 3% [S4], [S7], [S35].

| Stage | Yield, bn EUR | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Government announcement (PLF 2025) | ~2 (1.9 at the final vote) | [S4], [S35] |
| IPP mechanical estimate (2025 vintage) | 1.2 | IPP, June 2025 [S6] |
| IPP mechanical estimate updated with 2023 returns | 1.8 (or 1.2 if all PFU capital gains are treated as exceptional income) | IPP, June 2026 [S7] |
| **Observed December 2025 down-payment (meant to be 95% of the amount due)** | **< 0.4** | IPP citing the State's monthly accounts [S7] |
| 2026 government forecast | 0.7 (corrected 2026-10-25: was "0.65-0.7") | [S7], [S35] |
| HCFP: new 2026 measure "CDHR" | +0.4 | [S3] |

**Why this matters.** This is the cleanest recent French natural experiment on taxing the top. The yield appears to be about **20-35% of the announced amount**. The IPP lists possible explanations: calculation errors, deferral of capital gains, retaining dividends in holdings, conversion of dividends into salary, and pulling income forward into 2024. The number of theoretically liable households grew 27% between 2023 and 2024 returns, against 12% for households above the threshold but not liable [S7]. Household dividends **fell by 10 bn EUR in 2025** after rising 14 bn in 2024 (national accounts, cited by IPP [S7]). Some of these responses are timing effects that may reverse. The figures should therefore be read as "short-run yield far below static", not as a permanent elasticity.

## 5. How much of the deficit could "tax the rich" measures plausibly close?

This is the central question for the platform's thesis. The answer below is meant to be fair to both sides.

**Static upper bound (party claims).** LFI's 2027 package adds Zucman (15) + climate ISF (10) + flat-tax repeal (9) + inheritance (20) = **54 bn** of explicitly wealth- and capital-targeted revenue. Adding the "tax expenditures" line (22 bn), which hits businesses broadly (CIR, Dutreil), brings it to 76 bn [S18]. Even that maximal static figure is **35-50% of the 2025 deficit**, before behavior and before the 81.7 bn of new spending in the same package.

**Plausible post-behavior range (our synthesis).**

| Component | Plausible yield, bn EUR / yr | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Wealth tax on the very top (Zucman-type or ISF-type) | 3-10 | Between critics' ~5 and a heavily discounted author estimate. The ISF and Zucman bases overlap, so they cannot be added in full. |
| Capital-income taxation (end of the PFU, higher CSG on capital income) | 2-5 | Montaigne 3.2. The CDHR experience shows large timing and avoidance responses. |
| Top-income minimum tax (CDHR, already in place) | 0.5-1 | Observed |
| Inheritance-tax reform targeting large estates | 3-10 | No independent costing found. The base is large (~700,000 deaths a year), but a "top only" reform has a narrower base. |
| **Total** | **~10-25** | **≈ 7-16% of the 2025 deficit; ≈ 0.6-1.5% of public spending; ≈ 0.3-0.8% of GDP** |

**Honest conclusion.** "Tax-the-rich" measures could plausibly close **between one-fifteenth and one-sixth** of the deficit. That is not negligible: it is 2-6 times the yield of a full "année blanche" freeze (4.4-5.7 bn). But it is **not enough to balance the budget**, and it is of the same order as the **25 bn rise in interest costs between 2025 and 2027** [S3]. Closing the rest without touching spending would mean raising taxes broadly, which would push France's tax-to-GDP ratio, already the 2nd highest of 36 OECD countries (43.5% in 2024, OECD *Revenue Statistics*; not to be confused with Insee's *prélèvements obligatoires*, 43.6% of GDP in 2025), further up (see §6) (corrected 2026-10-25: was "2nd-highest PO in the OECD", which mixed the Insee and OECD concepts). Otherwise spending growth has to slow, mainly pensions (346 bn) and health (265 bn), which are the largest and most dynamic items [S3].

**The strongest left-wing arguments (steelman).**

1. **Tax cuts caused much of the hole.** The Cour des comptes estimates that tax and contribution cuts decided in 2018-2023 cost **62 bn in 2023 (2.2 pts of GDP)**, "not offset by spending savings", and contributed to the deficit [S11]. (Fairness note added after verification: the same page notes that the PO rate in 2022, at 45.0% of GDP, was still close to 2017's 45.3% because of strong spontaneous revenue growth in 2021-2022; the cuts showed up in the PO rate mainly in 2023.) On this reading, the problem is as much on the revenue side as the spending side. Restoring a fraction of that revenue is a legitimate part of consolidation. Rexecode (an employer-linked institute) reads the same history differently: in its *Document de travail* n° 105 (September 2026), levy cuts over 2014-2024 total 2.7 pts of GDP, of which **1.2 pts ("près de la moitié") have already been erased** by the increases since 2024, and cumulative measures since 2010 remain a net **increase** of about 2 pts of GDP (about 65 bn) (corrected 2026-10-25: was "66 bn current / 75 bn constant 2025 euros, roughly one-third already reversed (secondary)", which could not be found in a Rexecode publication) [S49].
2. **Billionaires' effective tax rates are regressive.** The IPP (note n° 92, June 2023, Bach, Bozio, Guillouzouic, Malgouyres, 2016 data, as summarized in the Senate report [S4], pp. 17-18) found that the total effective rate, all direct taxes included, falls from **46% for the top 0.1%** of tax households to **26% for the top 0.0002%** once undistributed profits of controlled firms are counted as income; personal taxes alone fall to **2%** of economic income at the very top, and the 26% includes corporate tax. The government disputes the method: its answer to written question n° 1212 calls the inclusion of undistributed profits "non consensuel" [S50]. The CDHR's own design confirms that some very high incomes paid below 20% [S6]. **Tax justice** is a valid goal regardless of yield. Blanchard's argument is that consolidation is politically impossible if transfers to modest households are cut while the rich optimize [S42].
3. **Exile is small.** The CAE (by IPP and CAE economists) finds that the 2013 capital-tax increase caused fewer than 350 additional net departures a year (upper bound), with a negligible macro effect [S5]. The CAE itself stresses that departures are "sensitive" to taxation, so the honest reading is "sensitive but small in absolute terms". This weakens the main objection to capital taxation.
4. **Spending is already being cut.** The HCFP notes that the 2027 adjustment is already spending-heavy against the trend (25 of 43 bn) [S3]. Without new revenue, the burden falls on pensioners, families and civil servants (regressive freezes, per the IPP [S6]).
5. **Wealth is concentrated and growing.** The top-500 fortunes rose from 124 bn (2003) to 1,228 bn (2024) [S4]. A tax base growing faster than GDP is an argument for taxing it.

**The strongest counter-arguments.**

1. **Behavior is first-order at the top.** The CDHR yielded roughly a quarter to a third of its announced amount [S7], and dividends shifted after the flat tax [S4]. A small, mobile, concentrated base makes yields fragile: three departures would halve the Zucman yield [S4].
2. **Legal risk.** A 2% uncapped wealth tax may be struck down [S4].
3. **Scale.** Even at full static yield, the main proposals cover about one-eighth of the deficit each. Programs that pair them with large new spending (the NFP pension package including a general return to 60: −58 bn, Montaigne; RN-type partial versions about 26-35 bn, see file 03) widen the deficit on net (Montaigne: NFP +179 bn, RN +71 bn) [S13].
4. **High existing tax burden.** France already has the 2nd-highest tax-to-GDP ratio in the OECD (43.5% in 2024) and the highest property-tax take in the EU-OECD group (3.4% of GDP) [S31]. The scope for broad-based increases is smaller than in most peers.

### 6.3 Outcome indicator

For this topic, the relevant "outcome" is **fiscal credibility**. France's sovereign risk premium widened in September 2026 (HCFP: "a marked rise ... in the risk premium specific to French debt"). France also "pays a remuneration as high as Italy's despite Italy's higher debt" [S3]. According to the **government's** estimate reported by the HCFP, a permanent +100 bp shock to all interest rates costs **+3.4 bn in year 1 and +8.1 bn in year 2** [S3] (corrected after verification: the figure is the government's, quoted by the HCFP, not an HCFP estimate). The HCFP adds that +1 pt of inflation raises the interest charge by about 3 bn the same year (inflation-linked bonds). A credible consolidation package of any composition affects debt service by an amount comparable to the yields of most single proposals in §4. *Spread series: see the debt/deficit file (06).*

## 8. Where economists disagree

1. **Elasticity of the top wealth base.** CAE/IPP economists (Bach, Bozio, Landais, Malgouyres) find small exile responses [S5]. A. Lévy, S. Catherine and P. Aghion argue that including business assets and targeting a tiny group change the response, and that avoidance (not exile) is the main channel [S4], [S26]. *Unresolved. The CDHR outturn [S7] supports the "large short-run avoidance" view for income-based taxes.*
2. **Should corporate tax count as tax paid by the owner?** Zucman: no, because retained earnings are untaxed personal income. Lévy and the Senate rapporteur: yes, otherwise it is a "sleight of hand" [S4]. The choice moves the yield by a factor of 7-10.
3. **Macroeconomic effects.** Zucman cites investment −0.07% and employment −0.05% [S4]. A Fondapol study (NiGEM-based, think tank critical of the tax) claims investment −18% and GDP −1.8%. That is an outlier with a contested method, so the platform should cite it only as an example of the range.
4. **Was the deficit caused by tax cuts or spending?** The Cour des comptes attributes a large part of the 2023-2024 deterioration to unfunded tax cuts (62 bn) [S11]. Others point to public spending at 57.3% of GDP in 2025, the 2nd highest of the EU-27 behind Finland in 2024 and 2025 (1st every year 2014-2023; Eurostat, file 00 row 6), and argue that revenue is already near the top (*prélèvements obligatoires* 43.6% of GDP in 2025; OECD tax-to-GDP 43.5% in 2024, 2nd of 36) (aligned 2026-10-25 with file 00: was "57% of GDP, the highest or near-highest in the EU"). *Both are arithmetically true. The disagreement is about which side should adjust.*
5. **Value of a temporary vs. permanent tax.** IPP: making the CDHR permanent makes deferral harder, so 2026 yields may rise [S7]. Others expect cumulative erosion.
6. **Credibility of "efficiency" savings.** Large savings claims from "inefficient spending", agencies and immigration (RN, LR) are seen by Fipeco/F. Ecalle, the Senate and the Institut de l'Entreprise as largely undocumented [S19], [S20]. The same skepticism applies to left-wing claims on fraud and windfall taxes (IFRAP on NFP [S41]).
