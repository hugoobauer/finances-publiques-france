## 0. Scope and definitions

**Scope.** This file covers the mandatory French pension system (*régimes de retraite légalement obligatoires*): basic schemes (*régimes de base*: CNAV, MSA, civil-service schemes, special schemes) plus mandatory complementary schemes (*régimes complémentaires*: Agirc-Arrco, Ircantec, etc.). It also covers the 2023 reform and its 2025-2026 "suspension", costings of a return to retirement at 60 or 62, the financing controversy over civil servants' pensions, demographics, and international comparisons.

**Key definitions (the source of most public confusion):**

| Term | Meaning | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| *Dépenses de retraite* (COR scope) | Gross spending of all mandatory schemes: direct-right pensions (*droits directs*), survivor pensions (*droits dérivés*/*réversion*), the old-age minimum (*Aspa*), management and social-action costs. Includes the FSV, excludes the RAFP. | This is the 14.1% of GDP headline. |
| COFOG GF1002 "Old age" (Eurostat/Insee) | National-accounts function of general government. Excludes survivors (GF1003), and disability (GF1001). | Gives a lower figure (13.4% of GDP in 2024). |
| ESSPROS "pensions" (Eurostat) | Social-protection pensions: old age, anticipated old age, survivors, disability pensions, early retirement. Includes **mandatory or quasi-mandatory private schemes** where they are classed as social protection (e.g., Dutch occupational funds). | Higher figure (15.1% of GDP for France in 2024). It narrows the gap with NL, DK, and SE. |
| OECD SOCX "public cash old-age and survivor benefits" | Public cash old-age plus survivor benefits. | 13.4% of GDP for France (2022). |
| Drees CPS *risque vieillesse-survie* | Social-protection accounts: pensions plus dependency allowances for the elderly (APA), accommodation help, and other old-age benefits. | 14.6% of GDP in 2024. It is broader than pensions alone. |
| Gross vs. net | Pensions bear the CSG, CRDS, CASA, and income tax. Net spending is lower. | COR: 14.1% gross vs. 13.2% net of levies (2025). OECD: 13.4% gross vs. 12.0% net (2022). |
| % of GDP vs. % of public spending | Pensions = 14.1% **of GDP** = 24.3% **of total public expenditure** (COR, 2025). | The platform must never mix the two. |
| *Âge d'ouverture des droits* (AOD) | Legal minimum age at which a pension can be claimed (62 to 64 under the 2023 reform). | Not the same as the effective age. |
| *Âge conjoncturel de départ* | Synthetic effective claiming age (Drees/COR). 63.1 years in 2025. | Different from the OECD "effective labour-market exit age" (61.9 men, 62.4 women in 2024), which measures leaving work, not claiming a pension. |
| *Ratio cotisants/retraités* | Contributors per retiree: 1.8 in 2025 (COR). | Not the same as the demographic ratio of people aged 20-64 to those 65+, which is 2.5 in 2025. |

**A rule of thumb for orders of magnitude:** French GDP in 2025 was about **2,990 bn EUR** (implied by COR: 422.2 bn EUR = 14.1% of GDP). So **1 point of GDP is about 30 bn EUR**.

## 2. Pension spending: level, structure, and trend

### 2.1 Level (2025, COR June 2026)

- Gross spending: **422.2 bn EUR = 14.1% of GDP = 24.3% of total public expenditure**. That compares with an average of 13.9% of GDP over the previous 10 years, with a peak of 14.7% in 2020 (when GDP fell sharply).[^cor26]
- Breakdown: 401 bn EUR of base and complementary pensions (excl. *Aspa*), of which **361 bn EUR are direct-right pensions (12.1% of GDP)** and **39 bn EUR are survivor pensions (1.3% of GDP)**. The rest, about 0.7% of GDP, is management, social action, and *Aspa*.[^cor26]
- **Net of levies** (CSG, CRDS, CASA, health contributions on complementary pensions): **394 bn EUR = 13.2% of GDP**.[^cor26]
- Share of public spending: pensions rose from **21.8% of public spending in 2002 to 24.7% in 2019**. The COR states that pensions explain "a large majority" of the increase in public spending relative to GDP over 2002-2019. Pension spending rose by 2 points of GDP; the COR's parenthesis also cites a 1.4-point figure for public spending. **[the exact reading of that parenthesis is ambiguous in the source; quote it rather than paraphrase]**[^cor26]
- Drees, 2026 *Les retraités et les retraites* data (end-2024): **17.3 million** direct-right pensioners (16.4 million living in France). About 18.2 million people receive some pension, including survivor-only pensioners. Average gross direct-right pension: **1,705 EUR/month** for residents of France, up 0.8% vs. 2023. Women's pensions are 36% lower than men's (25% lower once survivor pensions are included). There were 718,000 new retirees in 2024 (-7%). The average effective departure age was **63 years 7 months** in the general scheme and 63 years 2 months in the State civil-service scheme.[^dreesdata]

### 2.2 Projections (COR June 2026, reference scenario)

Assumptions: new Insee 2026 demographic scenario (fertility **1.45** from 2028, net migration +150,000 a year, slightly slower life-expectancy gains), long-run hourly productivity growth **0.7%**, unemployment **7.0%** from 2040.[^cor26]

**What changed vs. the June 2025 report:** spending is unchanged to 2045. In 2070 it is 1.1 points higher, driven by the fertility revision (+1.5 pt), partly offset by higher migration (-1.0 pt) and lower life expectancy (-0.2 pt). The LFSS 2026 measures, including the suspension, add 0.1 pt, and a new, more generous long-run Agirc-Arrco indexation convention adds 0.3 pt.[^cor26]

**COR's own message (important for balance):** the COR writes that these projections "do not corroborate the idea of pension spending escaping all control because of demographic ageing". It also writes that a 14% of GDP share remains "singularly high in international comparison" and that "the compass must remain the effect on public finances as a whole".[^cor26]

### 2.3 What holds spending down (COR's mechanics)

Spending relative to GDP is roughly (retirees / workers) x (average pension / average earnings). Over 2025-2070:[^cor26]
- Contributors: 30.6 million in 2025, rising until the late 2030s, then 28.9 million in 2070. Retirees (direct right): **17.4 million to 22.1 million**. The ratio falls from **1.8 to 1.3**.
- Average pension relative to average gross earnings: **54.6% to 45.3%**. Pensions grow 0.2% a year in real terms vs. 0.7% a year for earnings. That is a relative decline, not an absolute one: the average real pension in 2070 is 11% higher than today. The cause is price indexation of pensions and of past wages in the general scheme, lower Agirc-Arrco returns, and restrained public-sector pay.
- Effective claiming age: **63.1 years (2025) to 64.6 years**. It stabilizes in the 2040s, after which "life-expectancy gains are entirely devoted to longer retirement".

### 2.4 Time spent in retirement

- COR: retirement duration is about **24 years** for generations born in the late 1950s and early 1960s, a little under **28% of total lifetime**. It dips for generations born in the late 1960s (because of the AOD and contribution-length increases), then rises again with life-expectancy gains.[^cor26]
- Cour des comptes (Feb 2025): the average time in retirement falls to **23.5 years** for generations born 1965-1970, then rises back to **24.5 years** for the 1980 generation, the same as the 1940 generation.[^ccomptes25]
- Insee life-expectancy gap by income (2020-2024): men in the richest 5% live to **85.0** vs. **72.0** for the poorest 5%, a **13-year** gap. For women the figures are 88.7 vs. 80.1 (about 9 years). The gap widened slightly since 2012-2016.[^inseeEV] This is the core equity argument against uniform age increases.

### 2.5 Relative standard of living of retirees

- 2023: retirees' standard of living (per consumption unit) is **2,473 EUR/month vs. 2,468 EUR for the whole population**, i.e., about parity (**100.2%**). It peaked around 104% in 2013-2014. Including imputed rents (retirees mostly own their homes), retirees are **6.5% above** the population average.[^cor26]
- Projected: **90.3% in 2070**, a level similar to the early 1990s.[^cor26]
- Perception gap: in the Drees 2024 opinion barometer, **49%** of respondents think retirees are worse off than the population average, while only 20% think they are better off.[^cor26]
- The PLFSS 2027 states that pensions grew faster than wages in 2024 and 2025 and that retirees' saving rate rose faster than the general population's.[^plfss27]

### 3.2 What the suspension does (LFSS 2026)

Applies to pensions starting **on or after 1 Sep 2026**. The age ramp-up freezes for those born in 1964 and in Q1 1965, then resumes. AOD of 64 is reached for the **1969** generation instead of 1968. The 172-quarter requirement is reached for the **1966** generation instead of 1965. Active-category civil servants, the long-career scheme, and the permanent-disability early-exit scheme are shifted in parallel.[^cor26]

- For scale: the whole pension-system deficit is 5.1 bn EUR (2025), and the PLFSS 2027 pension-indexation savings are 4.1 bn EUR.

### 4.2 Short run vs. long run

- **Short run:** the cost ramps up generation by generation. A cut takes about 2-4 years to reach full effect (Cnav: 3.4 bn EUR in 2025, 15.7 bn EUR in 2032, then easing to 11.1 bn EUR in 2040 [^an613]; corrected 2026-10-25: was "about 16 bn EUR after 7 years").
- **Long run:** the cost stabilizes in % of GDP (Cour des comptes: "the effect rises quickly but stabilizes in the medium term"). Lower ages also reduce average pensions where the contribution-length rule binds (prorating and penalties), so part of the gross cost is offset for people without full careers.[^ccomptes25]
- **Macro feedback:** all models agree that a lower age reduces labor supply, GDP, and non-pension tax revenue. The DG Trésor long-run decomposition for **2 years of AOD**: pensions 0.4 pt, pension contributions 0.1 pt, other benefits -0.2 pt, other levies 0.6 pt, total **0.9 pt of GDP**.[^fipeco24] Short-run Keynesian models (Mésange, Insee/DG Trésor: 0.4 pt after 10 years; OFCE e-mod.fr: 0.15 pt; OFCE is the Sciences Po economic research center, generally Keynesian in orientation, and itself warns its model is designed for horizons under 5 years) find smaller effects.[^fipeco24]

## 5. Hidden financing? The civil servants' pensions and the "hidden deficit" controversy

### 5.1 The facts

- The State scheme for its civil and military servants (FPE) is booked in a **special allocation account** (*CAS Pensions*) that must balance. The State's employer contribution adjusts to make it balance. In 2023, the scheme paid 59.5 bn EUR of pensions; **75%** was financed by the State contribution (**45.1 bn EUR** in the Social Security accounts convention, or **7.6% of State spending**).[^ccomptes25]
- Apparent employer rates: **78.28%** for civilian servants (from 1 Jan 2025; 74.28% before) and **126.07%** for the military (since 2013 per the Cour des comptes; the COR RA 2026 says "depuis 2014": minor divergence between sources).[^ccomptes25] The civilian rate rose to **82.28%** on 1 Jan 2026 (+4 points).[^cor26] For comparison: 16.46% for private employers in 2023 (Cour des comptes) and 16.67% in 2026 (COR).
- In 2025, the State balancing contribution was **49.3 bn EUR**. Together with other special-scheme balancing subsidies (7.7 bn EUR), it amounted to about **1.9% of GDP**, falling to **1.1% of GDP in 2070** under the legal convention.[^cor26]
- Demographic ratio of the FPE scheme: **1.29** contributors per retiree vs. 2.05 for all schemes and 2.25 for the general scheme (IPP, 2020, full-career equivalents). On a direct-right basis, the Cour des comptes reports 1.02 vs. 1.52 for the general scheme (2023).[^cor26] [^ccomptes25]

### 5.2 View A: there is a large implicit deficit (30-55 bn EUR)

- **Bayrou (Jan 2025):** 380 bn EUR of pensions vs. 325 bn EUR of contributions plus earmarked taxes, a gap of **55 bn EUR a year**: 40-45 bn EUR from the State and 10-15 bn EUR from local authorities, about **1.8% of GDP** since the early 1990s.[^senat] This approach treats the State's contribution above a "private-sector" rate as a deficit.
- **Equivalent computation cited by the Cour des comptes:** with the general scheme's 16.46% employer rate as the benchmark, "over-contributions" for the three civil services amount to **42 bn EUR in 2023, of which 35 bn EUR for the State**.[^ccomptes25] The COR's January 2025 note is the reference given.
- **iFRAP** (Fondation iFRAP, free-market think tank) puts the State scheme's hidden deficit at **26.3 bn EUR in 2021**: State "over-contributions" above a 16.46% private-sector employer rate (38.4 bn EUR), minus solidarity spending (11.7 bn EUR) and excess contribution rates (0.4 bn EUR), built from Cour des comptes budget-execution notes (article of 27 Sep 2022). Note the data year (2021), older than the other estimates. (Confirmed 2026-10-25 on the iFRAP page itself; was "[secondary source, not opened] ⚠ unverified".)[^ifrap22]

### 5.3 View B: no hidden deficit; it is an accounting convention

- **Cour des comptes (20 Feb 2025):** "the comparison of rates to compute a possible State over-contribution is not possible". Reasons: (i) the contribution base excludes bonuses (22% of civilian pay, 36% of military pay; relative to total pay, the rate is about 58% for civilians and 80% for the military); (ii) active-category early exits; (iii) a degraded demographic ratio caused by the State's own control of headcount and pay; (iv) the State contribution mixes an employer contribution, non-contributory solidarity spending, and a balancing transfer. And above all, "the accounting of this public contribution has **no effect on the burden on public finances as a whole**".[^ccomptes25]
- **COR (June 2026):** the implicit rate "does not result from greater generosity" of public-sector rules. A Drees 2022 study found that applying private-sector rules to sedentary civil servants born in 1958 would have produced pensions **1.5% higher**. Under existing rules, demographic compensation between schemes gave the FPE scheme only 0.5 bn EUR in 2021, vs. about 11 bn EUR if the actual demographic imbalance were compensated. The privatization lump sums of France Télécom (5.7 bn EUR, 1997) and La Poste (2 bn EUR, 2002) were booked as immediate budget revenue, not reserves.[^cor26]
- **IPP (Aubert, Pedrono, Tô, Tochev, June 2025):** once demographic imbalance and solidarity schemes are neutralized (together worth **1.1% of GDP, 25.8 bn EUR**), the equilibrium employer rate would be **34.7%** (2020), about half the posted rate but well above the private rate.[^cor26] [^ipp25]
- **CAE (Focus n° 121, Sept 2025):** "no hidden deficit but an overstated wage cost". At a 34.7% rate, State employer contributions would be **21.5 bn EUR** (2023) instead of 50.4 bn EUR. The remaining **28.9 bn EUR** would be a transfer from the general budget. Booked that way, **total public spending and total public revenue would both fall by 28.9 bn EUR (about 1 pt of GDP)** with no change to the deficit. The CAE suggests the "fair" rate lies between **25.44%** (private rate adjusted for the bonus-free base) and 34.7%.[^cae121]

### 5.4 The COR's three conventions (how the "balance" changes with the convention)

| Convention | What it assumes | 2070 balance (reference) |
|---|---|---|
| **Legal convention** (*conforme à la législation*; earlier COR reports called it "EPR", *équilibre permanent des régimes*, and before that "CCSS", per COR working document n° 3 of 21 Apr 2022; the government's Jan 2023 report also uses "convention « EPR » du COR"[^cordoc22] [^rapport23]; confirmed 2026-10-25, was "from memory, unverified") | State and special schemes balanced each year; the State contribution falls with their spending. | **-2.4% of GDP** [^cor26] |
| **EEC** (*effort de l'État constant*, constant State effort; the EEC name has been used since 2022; earlier COR reports called it the "PIB" (GDP) convention. A third convention, TCC, *taux de cotisation constant*, formerly "COR5", was proposed for abandonment in April 2022 and does not appear in the 2026 report[^cordoc22]. Corrected 2026-10-25: was "earlier reports used 'EEE'", which was wrong) | State balancing contributions held at their 2021-2025 average share of GDP. | **-1.5% of GDP**; resources 13.7% of GDP vs. 12.9% [^cor26] |
| IPP / "hidden-deficit" type conventions | State contribution limited to a "normal" employer rate. | Much more negative today: the State scheme alone shows a deficit of about 1% of GDP (about 29 bn EUR at a 34.7% rate) up to about 1.2-1.8% (35-55 bn EUR at a private-sector rate). |

The COR notes that its legal convention is "intermediate" between EEC and IPP. **All three conventions leave the general-government deficit unchanged**: they only move the deficit between "pensions" and "the rest of the State".[^cor26]

**Platform takeaway:** the honest framing is not "hidden deficit: yes or no". Pensions cost 14.1% of GDP whatever the convention. The "balance" of the pension system is a convention, worth anywhere from -0.2% to about -1.9% of GDP today depending on which State transfers are counted as "resources". The COR itself recommends focusing on spending as a share of GDP and on the overall public balance.

**Steelman (in favor of the French model, against a naive comparison):**
1. **Funded pillars are not "free":** mandatory contributions to Dutch or Danish pension funds are economically close to taxes (they are compulsory and reduce disposable income). Eurostat's ESSPROS counts their benefits as social protection. Comparing only public spending overstates France's "excess".
2. **Tax expenditures:** the funded pillars are subsidized through the tax system. The Netherlands reports **1.9% of GDP** of tax breaks for retirement saving and Germany 1.0% (OECD Table 8.3, 2021). The OECD warns these figures are not comparable across countries.[^oecdpag]
3. **Transition cost:** moving from pay-as-you-go (PAYG) to funding requires one generation to pay twice (current pensions plus its own saving). It cannot reduce the burden quickly.
4. **Market risk:** funded systems shift financial and longevity risk onto households (e.g., Dutch funds cut benefits after 2008).
5. **Outcomes:** France's old-age poverty (6.1%) is comparable to DK (5.0%) and NL (4.6%), and far below DE (12.6%), which spends less publicly.

**Counter-steelman (in favor of the platform's thesis):** even counting private pillars, France's total (13.7% of GDP, OECD) is above NL (10.2), DK (9.5), SE (10.0), and DE (11.5). Within the panel, only Italy is higher (16.6%); OECD-wide, Greece (16.3%) and Austria (14.6%) are also higher (OECD PaG 2025, ch. 8 text). The remaining gap of 2-4 pts of GDP (60-120 bn EUR) is, **in our interpretation (not a source finding; the COR attributes part of France's high ratio to lower GDP per head)**, driven mostly by **earlier exit (61.9 vs. 65-66 for men) and a lower employment rate at 60-64 (44% vs. about 70%)**, not by the absence of funding.

## 9. Economists' disagreements and the strongest counter-arguments to the platform's thesis

The platform's thesis: proposals such as "return to 60" or "the rich will pay" ignore the structure of spending, and pensions are the single biggest item.

### 9.1 Steelman against the thesis

1. **"There is no runaway spending."** The COR: spending is stable at about 14.1-14.2% of GDP to 2045. The COR explicitly states this "does not corroborate the idea of pension spending escaping all control". The 2025 deficit is 0.2% of GDP, small relative to a general-government deficit of more than 5% of GDP.[^cor26]
2. **"The deficit is a choice about resources, not a spending problem."** Resources fall from 13.9% to 12.9% of GDP because State balancing contributions shrink by convention (1.9% to 1.1% of GDP) and because public-sector wage restraint cuts CNRACL contributions. Under the EEC convention, the 2070 deficit is -1.5% instead of -2.4%. The Cour des comptes estimates the current compensation rules for general contribution exemptions cost the old-age branch 2.2 bn EUR in 2024 (cited by the COR).[^cor26] Unions and the *Économistes atterrés* make this argument; the Ecologists' 2027 candidate cites "77 bn EUR of contribution exemptions".[^franceinfo26]
3. **"France spends a lot because its GDP per head is lower and its population older, not because pensions are lavish."** Per head, France ranks only 6th of the 11 countries the COR follows (OECD SOCX 2021, public plus private, USD PPP; behind the US, Italy, Sweden, Germany, and Belgium)[^cor26], and 5th of 27 in the EU (Eurostat ESSPROS pension expenditure per inhabitant, `spr_exp_pens`, 2024, EU-27 PPS; different country set, year and scope).[^es-esspros] Future replacement rates (70% net) are near the EU27 average (68.3%).[^oecdpag]
4. **"The outcome is excellent."** Old-age poverty of 6.1% vs. 14.8% for the OECD; retirees are less poor than the total population.[^oecdpag] Cutting pensions would raise poverty or shift costs to other budgets (social minima, family solidarity).
5. **"Raising the age is not a free lunch."** Macro models show a temporary rise in unemployment and spillovers to sickness, unemployment, and disability benefits. Drees/Dares put these at about **20% of the gross pension savings** for the 2010 reform.[^ccomptes25] The 13-year life-expectancy gap between the richest and poorest men makes uniform age increases regressive.[^inseeEV] Keynesian models (OFCE e-mod.fr) find a much smaller public-finance gain (0.15 pt of GDP after 10 years) than long-run models (0.9 pt).[^fipeco24]
6. **"The hidden-deficit story is accounting."** The Cour des comptes, the CAE, and the COR agree the State-contribution debate does not change the public balance.[^ccomptes25] [^cae121]
7. **"Lower pensions are also recessive."** COR simulations (DG Trésor, OFCE, I-MIP models, commissioned "pour la séance plénière de mars 2026", COR RA 2026 synthesis, p. 14; date confirmed 2026-10-25, was "⚠ unverified"): a 6 bn EUR pension moderation cuts GDP by 0.1-0.2%; higher contributions cost 15,000-80,000 jobs.[^cor26] (This argument cuts both ways: the same simulations find raising the age to be **expansionary**.)

### 9.2 Counter-arguments supporting the thesis

1. **Level and weight.** 14.1% of GDP and 24.3% of public spending (422 bn EUR). France is 2nd behind Italy among COR countries, 3rd-4th in the EU27, and 2nd in the panel on all three definitions.[^cor26] [^es-cofog] [^es-esspros]
2. **Long-run drift is real with current fertility.** Spending reaches 15.3% of GDP in 2070 and the deficit 2.4% of GDP (3.1% with 0.4% productivity). The system is in deficit **in every scenario**.[^cor26]
3. **Earliest exit in the panel.** Men's effective exit is 61.9, the lowest in the panel, and the 60-64 employment rate of 44% is also lowest. **Our interpretation (not stated by the sources):** this, more than demographics, explains the gap with NL, DK, SE, and DE.[^oecdpag] [^es-emp]
4. **The age lever is the only "expansionary" one.** In all three COR-commissioned models (DG Trésor, OFCE, I-MIP), one more year of AOD raises GDP by 0.1-0.8% and employment by 200,000-240,000 after 20 years, and improves the primary balance by 0.3-0.65 pt of GDP.[^cor26]
5. **Orders of magnitude vs. "tax the rich".** A general return to 60 costs **about 35-58 bn EUR a year (1.2-1.9 pt of GDP)**, and partial RN-type versions about 26-35 bn EUR (corrected after verification: was "26-58 bn EUR a year (0.9-1.9 pt of GDP)"). Even a return to 62 (10-17 bn EUR) exceeds the entire 2025 pension-system deficit (5.1 bn EUR) by a factor of 2-3. *(Cross-reference: compare with the yield estimates in the wealth-tax research file.)*
6. **Retirees' relative position.** Retirees' standard of living equals the population's (100.2%), rising to 106.5% with imputed rents. The government's comité de suivi des retraites recommended acting on indexation in 2025 and again in 2026.[^cor26] [^plfss27]

### 9.3 Where economists genuinely disagree

- **Size of the public-finance gain from age increases:** 0.15 pt (OFCE, 10 yrs) vs. 0.4 pt (Mésange, 10 yrs) vs. 0.9 pt (DG Trésor long run, or micro-econometric evidence from the 2010 reform). The Cour des comptes leans toward the micro-econometric evidence: after the 2010 reform, the probability of unemployment for those already in work rose only from 1.2% to 1.7%.[^ccomptes25]
- **Accounting of the civil servants' scheme:** Bayrou / iFRAP / some Plan-commission work vs. Cour des comptes / CAE / IPP / COR.
- **Whether the indicator should be spending or balance:** the COR, the European Commission, and the OECD prefer spending in % of GDP. The political debate focuses on the "balance".
- **The welfare value of leisure:** the COR notes that a higher age raises GDP but reduces "free time". The net welfare effect depends on preferences, education, and job type (Martinez and Perona, Cepremap 2026).[^cor26]
