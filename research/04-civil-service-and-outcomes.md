# 04 - Civil service: headcount, wage bill, international comparison, and public service outcomes

*Research file for the public-finance education platform. Prepared 2026-10-25. All web sources accessed 2026-10-25 unless stated otherwise. Figures computed by us from raw data are marked "(our computation)" and the underlying CSV is cited.*

## 0. Bottom line (for the platform editors)

1. **France has a large public workforce, but not an outlier one.** 5.85 million agents at end-2024 (5.88 million including subsidized contracts), about 1 job in 5. On the OECD national-accounts definition, general government employment is 20.7% of total employment (2023). That is 8th of 30 OECD countries, above the OECD average (18.4%), and far below Sweden (28.1%) and Denmark (27.3%). It is far above Germany (11.5%) and the Netherlands (12.3%). The French share has fallen since 2019 (-0.9 pt).
2. **The "wage bill" headline (12.4% of GDP, 21.6% of public spending in 2025; EU27 aggregate 10.3% of GDP, euro area EA21 aggregate 10.0% of GDP, matching file 00 row 18) needs three caveats before any comparison is drawn:**
   - Measured as a **% of total public expenditure**, France's wage bill is *below* the EU median (rank 22/27). Measured as a **% of GDP**, it is above (rank 6/27).
   - Measured as **gross wages and salaries (D.11)**, France is *below* the EU median: 8.3% of GDP against 8.9%. The gap in total compensation comes from employer social contributions, including imputed civil-service pension contributions.
   - Once **make-or-buy** is neutralized, France (24.3% of GDP) is level with Germany (24.1%), Sweden (24.1%), Belgium (25.0%) and the Netherlands (26.1%). Make-or-buy here means adding the government's own staff, its purchases, and the services it buys from private or non-profit providers (e.g. German and Dutch hospitals).
3. **Outcomes are mixed, not uniformly bad.** France is excellent on treatable mortality (3rd lowest in the EU), on low out-of-pocket health costs (3rd lowest) and on early-childhood enrollment. It is mediocre to poor on school results, especially in primary mathematics (TIMSS 2023: 484 against an EU average of 524), and now below the OECD average in PISA 2025 mathematics and reading (458 and 456 vs. 463 and 461; added after verification). In mathematics France ranks **29th of 38 OECD countries** (PISA 2025; file 00 row 49, file 13 §4.1). It is also weak on judicial capacity (11 judges per 100,000 inhabitants against an EU median of 17). It is also weak on digital public services and on perceived government effectiveness, which is well below its 2005 level (corrected after verification: was "declining since 2005"; the World Bank score fell in two steps, 2005-06 and 2023-24, with a plateau from 2010 to 2023).
4. **Platform thesis check.** The data support "more spending does not automatically mean better outcomes": school spending is at or above the OECD average while results have deteriorated. The data contradict a naive "too many civil servants" story. The French specificity lies in how staff are paid and financed (social contributions, pensions), in how they are allocated (secondary vs. primary, administration vs. front line), and in low pay for some key professions (teachers, judges). It does not lie in an extreme headcount. The steelman in Section 8 should be presented to users.

Key dynamics:
- **Contract staff drive all net growth.** In 2024 the number of tenured civil servants (incl. military) was stable (-0.1%, -3,900), while contract staff rose 2.6% (+35,900) [S2]. Contract staff were 74% of new entrants. The tenured balance of entries minus exits was -104,200 [S2].
- **For the first time, category A agents outnumber category C** (38% vs. 37%). This comes partly from reclassifications (e.g. nurses and some care staff moved up a category) and partly from real upskilling [S2].
- **Turnover:** 528,800 entries and 499,400 exits in 2024 (civilians), a 9.3% rotation rate [S2]. The DGAFP's 2026 report gives 517,100 / 481,600 for the civil service on its own perimeter [S1]. This is a source divergence: the perimeters differ.
- **Working time:** full-time agents excluding teachers declare 1,623 hours of actual work a year (FPE 1,660), which is 57 hours less than the private sector (2025 data) [S1].

Context that matters for interpretation:
- About **140,000 agents moved from the state to local authorities in 2006-2010** (2004 decentralization law). Branch-level trends must be read with this in mind. Fipeco adjusts for it [S3].
- The **35-hour week** (1998-2002) explains part of the 1997-2007 FPT and FPH hiring wave, e.g. FPH +2.5%/yr in 1999-2004 [S3].

**Reading guide for users:**
- **% of GDP**: France spends 2.1 pts of GDP more than the EU27 aggregate on public employee compensation. That is about EUR 63 bn at 2025 GDP (our computation: 2.1% x about EUR 2,990 bn GDP, which is implied by 1,714.1 bn / 57.3%).
- **% of total public expenditure**: the wage bill is a *smaller* share of French public spending than in the median EU country. France's excess public spending is concentrated elsewhere (social benefits, pensions; see files 01-03).
- **D.1 vs. D.11**: France's gross wages and salaries (8.3% of GDP) are *below* the EU median. Most of the French gap in D.1 comes from **employer social contributions**. For France, D.1 minus D.11 = 4.1 pts of GDP, against 2.1 in Germany, 2.7 in Italy (corrected after verification: was 3.1; 9.0 - 6.3 = 2.7), 0.4 in Denmark. A large part is the **imputed pension contribution for state civil servants** and military personnel. This is partly a financing convention, not "more civil servants". The platform should not present D.1 alone as evidence of overstaffing.

### 5.2 Caveats (must accompany any chart)

1. **Public vs. private provision.** In Germany and the Netherlands, most hospitals and many social services are operated by non-profit or private entities financed by social insurance. Their staff are not "government employees". In France, about 1.25 million hospital and medico-social workers are civil servants (FPH). The OECD text says so explicitly: "in some countries the majority of healthcare professionals, teachers and emergency responders are public employees, whereas in others these roles are primarily filled by workers in private or non-profit organisations operating within publicly funded systems" [S5]. The COFOG table in Section 4.3 quantifies this for hospitals.
2. **Schools.** French private schools under contract are mostly state-paid: their teachers are counted in the FPE. Comparable schools elsewhere (e.g. Dutch publicly funded private schools) may be classified differently. Not quantified here (gap).
3. **Outsourcing.** Cleaning, IT, catering and similar services may be in-house or purchased (P.2). Section 4.4 neutralizes this.
4. **Perimeter divergence within France.** The OECD (national accounts S.13) gives 20.7% (2023). DGAFP/Insee (legal civil-service perimeter, incl. subsidized contracts) gives 19.8% (2024). Fipeco (excl. subsidized contracts, persons in employment) gives 19.2% (2024) [S1][S3][S5]. See Section 9.

## 8. Economists' disagreements and the steelman against the platform's thesis

### 8.1 The thesis ("the structure of spending must be questioned; more means do not equal better outcomes")

Evidence in favor:
- **Education:** spending per student is at the OECD level and the number of primary teachers grew 6% while pupils fell 4% (2016-2024) [S10], yet results fell sharply (PISA maths -37 pts from 2003 to 2022 and a further -16 pts to 458 in 2025, now below the OECD average; TIMSS primary bottom of the EU) [S12][S12b][S13]. More resources did not stop the decline.
- **Administration:** general public services spending and staff (COFOG GF01 compensation 1.6% of GDP, rank 7/27) [S9] coexist with a government-effectiveness score well below its 2005 level [S27] and lagging digital public services [S28]. *Caveat (added after verification):* GF01 *total* spending (6.2% of GDP) includes 2.0 pts of debt interest. Excluding interest, France (4.2%) is close to the EU27 aggregate (4.1%) [S23].
- **Cour des comptes (Sept 2026 note, verified against the original PDF on 2026-10-25):** bringing the deficit below 3% of GDP by 2029 requires an overall effort of at least EUR 80 bn. If the wage bill contributed in proportion to its weight (21.7% of spending), that would mean **EUR 17.4 bn of wage-bill savings in full-year 2032**. Because other priorities (defense, ecological transition, health, long-term care) will need more money, the Cour says the wage-bill effort should be **"at minimum around EUR 20 bn"**, over the note's projection horizon to 2032 (the note costs its scenarios as savings in 2032 against the spontaneous trend). It names headcount reduction (non-replacement of retirees in the State, a return of local-government staff to the 2014 level, i.e. -140,000 agents) as the priority lever, plus a multi-year under-indexation of the index point, targeted category measures and slower career progression [S6]. (Corrected 2026-10-25: was "about EUR 20 bn of savings over three years ... if it contributed in proportion to its weight". The "three years" came from the AFP dispatch; the note's horizon is 2032, and the proportional share is 17.4 bn, not 20 bn.) The IMF (2024 Article IV concluding statement, 23 May 2024) argued that "the increase in the public sector salary bill can be contained by reducing the overlaps between different levels of government and through a greater reliance on automation and digitalization" [S29] (quote verified; the S29 link was corrected, see Section 11). The OECD *Economic Survey of France 2026* states that "improving public spending efficiency is essential to ensure fiscal sustainability, while providing space for upcoming defence and growth-enhancing spending such as education" (OECD press release, 30 Jun 2026) [S30] (corrected 2026-10-25: was the paraphrase "greater spending efficiency", presented as a quote; the wording above was checked on the OECD press release page in a browser).
- **Working time:** 1,623 hours a year actually worked vs. 57 more in the private sector [S1]. The 35-hour transition explains part of the 1999-2004 hiring wave [S3].
- **Local government:** the FPT is the fastest-growing branch since 1997 (+47%) despite transfers [S3]. This is often linked to the overlapping local layers (*millefeuille territorial*); that explanation is a common argument, not quantified here.

### 8.2 Steelman: the strongest counter-arguments (to be presented fairly)

0. **"Public employment has grown in line with employment, not faster."** (Added after verification, from [S3].) Fipeco's own series shows headcount up 25% from 1997 to 2024 (22% if subsidized contracts are included), against +23% for persons in employment and +28% for total jobs. The civil-service share of persons in employment was 19.7% in 1997 and 19.2% in 2024. The "+25% vs. population +15%" comparison is accurate, but the population denominator flatters the thesis: employment is the more relevant benchmark for a share of the labor force. Fipeco is generally seen as fiscally orthodox, which makes this a concession from a source sympathetic to the thesis.
1. **"France does not have too many civil servants; it chose public provision."** Germany's and the Netherlands' low public employment shares (11.5% and 12.3%) mostly reflect hospitals and social services run by non-profit or private providers paid by social insurance [S5]. Once own production, purchases and transfers in kind are added up (Section 4.4), France (24.3% of GDP) is at the level of Germany (24.1%) and below the Netherlands (26.1%) [S4]. Cutting civil servants would partly shift costs to purchased services rather than eliminate them. *(Verification note: on this production-cost measure France still ranks 4th/27 and sits 3.2 pts above the EU median (21.1%). The argument neutralizes the gap with the northern panel, not with the EU as a whole.)*
2. **"The French public wage bill is mainly a financing convention."** Gross wages and salaries (D.11) are 8.3% of GDP in France, *below* the EU median of 8.9% [S4]. The D.1 gap comes from employer contributions, especially imputed state pension contributions. That is a pension-financing question (see file 02), not a staffing one.
3. **"Public pay is low where it matters most."** French teachers earn about 18% less than the OECD average at 15 years of experience in lower secondary (USD 54,778 vs. 66,583), and rank 27th of 37 OECD systems [S11]. They earn 10-18% less than other tertiary graduates [S10]. Only 27% are satisfied with their salary [S10]. Public-sector *cadres* earn 17% less than their private-sector counterparts [S1]. Reasonable economists (and the Haut-commissariat à la Stratégie et au Plan) link the teaching profession's loss of attractiveness to falling results [S15]. In this view, the problem is under-investment in human capital, not over-spending.
4. **"Some sectors are objectively under-resourced."**
   - *Justice:* 11 judges per 100,000 vs. an EU median of 17 and 26 in Germany [S22]. Civil cases take 434 days at first instance [S24].
   - *Health access:* physician density is below the EU median [S17]. Beds fell 26% between 2005 and 2024 [S17]. ER stays lengthened by 55 minutes in ten years [S19]. Unmet needs tripled between 2019 and 2025 [S17].
   - *Primary education:* per-student spending is about 10% below the OECD average [S10].
   These points support targeted *re*-investment, not across-the-board cuts.
5. **"Outcomes reflect more than public spending."** Life expectancy, homicide rates and test scores depend on demographics, social inequality, immigration and diet. The PISA social gradient is particularly strong in France (21.5% of variance explained vs. 15.5% in the OECD) [S12]. Simple means-to-results ratios overstate what spending alone can achieve.
6. **"Health is a French success story for the money."** Treatable mortality is 3rd lowest in the EU and out-of-pocket spending is 3rd lowest [S17]. Cuts there carry real outcome risk.
7. **Measurement of public productivity.** In national accounts, non-market output is largely measured by its inputs (costs). This makes "efficiency" comparisons from aggregate statistics fragile (a standard methodological point; no specific French source retrieved).
